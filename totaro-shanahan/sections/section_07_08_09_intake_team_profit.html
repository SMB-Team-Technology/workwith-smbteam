Intake Assessment

Intake was not discussed in depth on the call, and today's Chapter 7 volume is low — but the goal of 20–30 cases a month is ten times what comes in now or more. Fixing intake means converting more of the leads the firm is already generating — without spending another dollar on marketing.

Status: AMBER
"we'd like to get this chapter sevens up to about 20 or 30 a month."
The Problem
  • No dedicated intake role was mentioned for a three-person firm, so new Chapter 7 calls would reach the same few people already running the practice
  • Moving from about two Chapter 7 cases a month to 20–30 needs a defined process for screening, scheduling, and following up — none was described on the call
  • Michael generally steers Chapter 13 inquiries toward Chapter 11 — a judgment call that will need to be written down so others can apply it as volume grows
Blind Spot: People searching for bankruptcy help are often in a stressful moment and contacting several firms. A slow or missed response may send the call to a competitor advertising a free consultation.
The Positive
  • Chapter 7 is quick work for Michael — he described doing one in about an hour and a half — so a well-qualified inquiry can turn into a signed case fast
  • The website already gives visitors a clear next step with "Get Free Consultation" buttons and a contact form
The Gap
  • At about $2,000 per Chapter 7 case, every missed or slow-handled inquiry is revenue handed to a competitor
  • Without a defined follow-up sequence, people who do not sign on the first call are likely to be lost to the next firm they contact
  • Marketing can bring 20–30 cases a month to the front door, but they only convert if the front door can handle them
A defined intake process lets new Chapter 7 clients come in without every call landing on Michael's desk — protecting the time he wants to spend on legal work.

Website Contact Forms & Live Chat

  • A contact form sits in the hero area and again near the bottom of the page, alongside "Get Free Consultation" and "Explore Your Options" buttons at the top
  • No chat widget or other after-hours capture tool was observed on the site
  • There is no video or educational content on the site to reassure a visitor before they reach out

Team Assessment

Michael carries the legal work, the administration, and the marketing decisions for a three-person firm, and he has said he wants to hand more of that off. Until this changes, the owner cannot have more free time — no matter how much revenue grows.

Status: AMBER
"I want to turn that around and spend more time on the legal work, and I'm also trying to expand the administrative aspect to somebody else. So that we can actually grow."
The Problem
  • A three-person firm — two attorneys, one of whom works remotely, and one paralegal — has little room to absorb 20–30 new cases a month
  • Michael personally carries the casework, the administration, and the decisions about how the firm grows
  • No one owns marketing today, and Michael has said he would rather have someone else handle it than learn it himself
  • Ten times the Chapter 7 volume means ten times the client communication, document collection, and filing work behind each case
Blind Spot: Chapter 7 is fast work for Michael, which can make it feel easy to take on more. At 20–30 a month, though, it is the work around each case that will need more hands.
The Positive
  • Michael is clear about what he wants to delegate — marketing and administration — and what he wants to keep: the legal work
  • A small, experienced team with decades of Chapter 11 specialization and long-standing referral relationships is a strong base to grow from
The Gap
  • Every hour Michael spends on administration or marketing is an hour not spent on the legal work he said he wants more of
  • Without someone owning growth, marketing decisions stall or default back to him
  • Scaling Chapter 7 volume without added help risks reaching the same ceiling — with more work on the same few shoulders
Putting marketing and administration in someone else's hands is what gives Michael his time back for the legal work.

Profit Plan Assessment

Revenue has rebuilt from a COVID low to about $600,000 a year and is still rising, and Michael is targeting $1.5 million next year. Revenue is vanity, profit is sanity. Until there is a clear profit plan, growth may just mean more work without more wealth.

Status: AMBER
"I think we're now back up to about $600,000, but that's increasing."
The Problem
  • Revenue has swung widely in recent years — about $900,000 before COVID, a low near $200,000, and about $600,000 now
  • Michael said he has no benchmark for what marketing should cost or return — "I don't have any base to go from" — so there is no yardstick to plan spend against the $1.5 million goal
  • Chapter 11 revenue depends on referrals from other attorneys, which the firm cannot control or scale on demand
  • No digital marketing spend or return tracking exists today, because no digital campaigns have run
Blind Spot: Chapter 7 cases at about $2,000 each and Chapter 11 cases at $20,000–$50,000 behave very differently as a marketing investment. Tracking what each Chapter 7 costs to win, from day one, is what keeps 20–30 cases a month a plan instead of a guess.
The Positive
  • Flat fees collected up front give the firm a clean, simple cash model to build on
  • Revenue is trending up, and Michael has a specific target and a specific path to it: 20–30 Chapter 7 cases a month on top of the Chapter 11 work
The Gap
  • Without a target cost per Chapter 7 case, there is no way to know how much marketing can be spent with confidence
  • With one dominant lead source, any slowdown in referrals hits revenue directly — the recovery from the COVID low shows how much the firm depends on it
  • A number-by-number plan from today's $600,000 to $1.5 million does not yet exist
Knowing what each new case costs and returns lets Michael grow with confidence — and hand the marketing to someone else without losing sight of the numbers.
