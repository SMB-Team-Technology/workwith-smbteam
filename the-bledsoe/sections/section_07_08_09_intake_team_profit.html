Intake Assessment

The Bledsoe Firm converts only 17% of leads into consultations, even with a $200 paid-consultation model already filtering out low-intent prospects. Fixing intake means converting more of the leads the firm is already generating. This is where the biggest immediate ROI lives.

Status: AMBER
The Problem
  • A 17% lead-to-consultation close rate means roughly five of every six leads the firm pays for never convert
  • No defined process was described for grading calls, coaching staff on objection handling, or following up with leads who don't book immediately
  • The primary contact form sits near the footer instead of above the fold, and there is no live chat for after-hours visitors
  • Marketing and intake ownership is only now being handed to Kate — a new transition with no established intake SOP yet in place
Blind Spot: You may not realize how many of the visitors your $36,000/month in marketing brings to the site never see a form, never get a callback, and quietly call a competitor instead.
The Positive
  • The firm already uses a $200 paid consultation model as a deliberate filtering mechanism — a real system, not an accident, and a foundation to build on rather than start from scratch
The Gap
  • At a 17% close rate, doubling conversion would mean retaining twice the clients from the exact same ad spend already committed — real revenue currently left on the table
  • Without a defined follow-up sequence, leads who don't sign on the first call are likely lost permanently rather than nurtured
  • The current record month of 17 new clients — against a goal of 20+ — is being capped by conversion, not by lead volume, since $36,000/month is already generating substantial traffic
Doubling the close rate is the single fastest path to the client volume John needs to justify opening San Diego and LA — without spending another marketing dollar.

Website Contact Forms & Live Chat

  • No live chat is present anywhere on the site
  • The phone number in the header is the only above-the-fold contact method, and it does not match the number listed on multiple third-party directories

Team Assessment

John is delegating marketing and intake ownership to Kate for the first time, after personally directing the firm through a tripling in size. Until this changes, the owner cannot have more free time — no matter how much revenue grows.

Status: AMBER
The Problem
  • John is delegating marketing and intake to Kate, but this is a brand-new transition with no track record yet at the firm's current scale — let alone the planned San Diego and LA offices
  • No additional dedicated ops, marketing, or intake staff member beyond Kate has been described, even as vendor complexity has grown to four active engagements
  • Managing four separate vendor relationships (Noble, direct mail, Vertical 10, Google Ads) still appears to run through John and Kate directly, with no confirmed consolidated reporting layer
  • No leadership or management structure has been described for the two new offices being planned in San Diego and LA
Blind Spot: Delegating marketing and intake to Kate is the right move — but doing it before the underlying systems are consolidated risks handing her the same fragmentation John has been managing, just under a different name.
The Positive
  • John is proactively choosing to delegate rather than waiting until he burns out — a mindset shift most owners at this stage haven't made yet, and exactly the foundation Stage 6 is built on
The Gap
  • Every vendor relationship John or Kate has to personally manage is time neither of them is spending on strategy or the new office launches
  • Without a proven operational layer, opening two new offices risks tripling the current coordination burden instead of freeing anyone up
  • If Kate's transition stalls under the weight of four disconnected vendors, John may find himself pulled back into day-to-day marketing oversight right when he's trying to step away
Consolidating four vendors into one accountable partner is what actually lets Kate own this — and lets John focus on opening San Diego and LA instead of managing agencies.

Profit Plan Assessment

No case values, acquisition costs, or profitability figures were discussed on the call, even though the firm invests $36,000 a month across four vendors and is targeting new-market expansion. Revenue is vanity, profit is sanity. Until there is a clear profit plan, growth may just mean more work without more wealth.

Status: AMBER
The Problem
  • No average case value was stated, so the true return on $36,000/month in marketing spend cannot currently be measured
  • No cost-per-acquisition or channel-level profitability tracking was mentioned across any of the four active vendors
  • No revenue or profit goal was stated in dollar terms — growth is measured in client volume ("double again"), not in what that volume returns to the firm
  • Opening two new offices without current channel profitability data means expansion costs could outpace the return they generate
Blind Spot: Tripling in size does not necessarily mean tripling in profit — without knowing case value and acquisition cost by channel, John cannot know whether the $36,000/month is being spent where it returns the most, or simply where it always has been.
The Positive
  • The firm's growth — tripling in size, a record month of 17 new clients — shows genuine top-line momentum, a real foundation to build profit visibility on top of rather than create from nothing
The Gap
  • Without knowing case value, the $36,000/month spend cannot be validated as profitable versus merely productive
  • Two new offices funded without clear per-channel return data risks scaling costs faster than scaling profit
  • John cannot make a confident go/no-go decision on San Diego and LA without knowing what his current location actually returns per marketing dollar
Real financial visibility — not just client volume — is what lets John expand into San Diego and LA on data instead of momentum alone.
