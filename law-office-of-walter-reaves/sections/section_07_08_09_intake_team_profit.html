Intake Assessment

Walter and one part-time legal assistant are personally fielding every incoming call for the firm. Fixing intake means converting more of the leads the firm is already generating. This is where the biggest immediate ROI lives.

Status: RED
"Cannot manage all incoming calls" — per the call summary, describing the part-time legal assistant's intake capacity.
The Problem
  • Walter and one part-time assistant are the only two people fielding every incoming call — no delegation beyond that exists
  • No documented follow-up sequence exists for leads who do not retain the firm on the first call
  • No after-hours coverage plan is in place beyond whatever Walter or the assistant can personally catch
  • The firm is losing an estimated 5–6 cases a month — roughly $20,000–$24,000 in monthly revenue — because no one can answer fast enough
Blind Spot: You may not realize how many of those 5–6 lost cases a month are calls that reached someone at the firm but simply were not followed up on again — not calls that were never answered at all.
The Positive
  • Walter's personal involvement in intake means every prospective client who does reach him gets a genuine, credible first impression from a 45-year board-certified attorney — not a call screener
The Gap
  • An estimated $20,000–$24,000 a month in lost revenue — money the firm cannot spare while rebuilding after the merger
  • Every unanswered or unfollowed-up call is a prospective client who may retain Dunham & Jones, Dunnam & Dunnam, or Russell D. Hunt Sr. instead
  • There is currently no way to know how many leads are being lost at each stage of the process — only the aggregate estimate
Fixing intake is the fastest way to slow the bleeding — it costs nothing in new ad spend and starts recovering revenue immediately.

Website Contact Forms & Live Chat

  • The contact form is not buried — it appears above the fold and twice more down the page
  • No live chat is present, so every after-hours visitor must either call, fill out the form and wait, or leave
  • There is no confirmation of how quickly form submissions are currently followed up on — this should be timed and tracked going forward

Team Assessment

The entire practice runs through two people: Walter and one part-time legal assistant, with no one else positioned to keep things moving if either is unavailable. Until this changes, the owner cannot have more free time — no matter how much revenue grows.

Status: AMBER
The Problem
  • Team size is two: Walter (sole decision-maker and attorney) and one part-time legal assistant
  • There is no accountability structure or org chart — every decision runs through Walter
  • The part-time assistant is already overloaded and "cannot manage all incoming calls," per the call summary
  • There is no one cross-trained to cover case strategy, intake, or client communication if Walter is out
Blind Spot: After 45 years of being the practice, it may not feel unusual that everything runs through you — but that is precisely what keeps the firm's revenue tied to your personal capacity instead of a system that can grow past it.
The Positive
  • The part-time assistant relationship is an existing foundation to build structure on top of — the firm is not starting from zero
The Gap
  • Walter cannot take meaningful time away from the practice without risking lost calls and lost cases
  • Every hour spent on intake and administration is an hour not spent on casework or rebuilding the client base
  • The firm's post-merger recovery depends entirely on one person's bandwidth, with no backup if that bandwidth runs out
Until someone besides Walter can carry part of the load, there is no path to more free time — only more hours.

Profit Plan Assessment

A failed merger cut this firm's annual revenue roughly in half, from about $600,000 to about $250,000, and there is no current profit plan guiding the rebuild. Revenue is vanity, profit is sanity. Until there is a clear profit plan, growth may just mean more work without more wealth.

Status: RED
The Problem
  • Annual revenue dropped from about $600,000 to about $250,000 following a failed merger
  • Current marketing spend (~$800/month, self-managed) reflects a real, stated budget crisis, not a strategic choice
  • No cost-per-case or margin tracking exists to show which parts of the practice are actually profitable post-merger
  • An estimated $20,000–$24,000 a month is being lost to intake gaps alone, compounding the revenue pressure
Blind Spot: The pressure of a budget crisis can push toward cutting the wrong things — without visibility into cost-per-case, it is just as easy to cut something that would have helped the rebuild as something that would not have.
The Gap
  • Without cost-per-case visibility, every rebuilding decision is being made on instinct instead of numbers
  • The firm cannot yet set a specific, trackable revenue-recovery target — only a general sense of urgency
  • There is no early-warning system to show whether the practice is stabilizing or still declining month to month
Rebuilding deliberately — toward a specific number, not just away from the pressure — is what turns this from a crisis into a plan.
